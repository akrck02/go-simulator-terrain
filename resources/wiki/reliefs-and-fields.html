🏔️ Reliefs and fields

Almost every simulator needs to know the shape of the land: the climate to know where it is cold, the forests to know where it is too steep, the town to know where it can build. Instead of each one loading its own copy of the relief (and maybe disagreeing about it), the terrain plugin reads it once and puts it in the world for everyone.

A made-up island as the other plugins see it: the height of every cell, and how steep it is. Drawn from a real run (see how the pictures were made).

Put it first in the rules file: plugins start in order, so the ones after it find the ground already there.

What it publishes

At the start, two fields of the vocabulary:

Field Unit How
elevation metres the relief’s heights, untouched
slope rise over run the gradient like numpy.gradient: central differences inside, one-sided at the edges

Values sit at cell centres, rows from the smallest z and columns from the smallest x. The relief rule publishes them once: on its own the ground does not change, so later steps publish nothing. To let it change, add the erosion rule after it: the land rises, the rivers cut their valleys and the hillslopes round off, and the rule publishes the ground again as it changes, with drainage_area and the rivers.

How the slope is measured

Every cell gets two rises over run, one along the row (x) and one along the column (z), taken like numpy.gradient: half the difference between the two neighbours inside the grid, and the difference with the only neighbour at the edges. The slope is the length of those two, √(along_x² + along_z²). The numbers in the picture are what the plugin’s own code gives for that row.

The distance between cells is in metres, so the slope is a true rise over run: the cell size in world units times metres_per_unit (1000 by default, for scenes in kilometres). A slope of 1 is a 45° flank.

One row of the island's two fields, along the dashed line A–B in the picture at the top. The slope is highest on the flanks and drops on the rounded summit, where the neighbours on both sides are lower.

Formats

The rule tells the two apart by the end of path: .json or .npy.

go-scene-builder’s relief

relieve.json, {"columns", "rows", "cell", "west", "north", "units"}, with the heights in metres as little-endian float32 in relieve.f32 next to it, row by row from the north. Its coordinates are kilometres east and north; origin places it on the world: x = east − origin[0], z = origin[1] − north. Rows go from the north, which is the smallest z, so no row is flipped.

- rule: relief
  with: {path: relieve.json, origin: [6, 4.5]}

NumPy array

A two-dimensional .npy of float32 or float64 heights in metres, rows from the smallest z, placed by the centre of its first cell and the spacing of its cells, in world units.

- rule: relief
  with: {path: relief.npy, minimum_x: -1, minimum_z: -1, cell_size: 0.0125}

metres_per_unit (1000 for scenes in kilometres) turns the cell size into metres for the slope.

The island in these pictures was written both ways, as a relieve.json with origin: [4, 2.75] and as a .npy with minimum_x: -3.975, minimum_z: -2.725 and cell_size: 0.05; the two runs published byte-identical fields.

Exactness

The field’s extent is float64, so a plugin that rebuilds the grid from the field gets the same cell size and cell centres as the file. This matters for simulations that are sensitive to their inputs: a tenth of a millimetre of difference in the heights can grow a different town.
